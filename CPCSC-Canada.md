# Canadian Program for Cyber Security Certification (CPCSC)

**Framework:** Canadian Program for Cyber Security Certification  
**Country:** Canada  
**Responsible Government Department:** Public Services and Procurement Canada (PSPC)  
**Technical Authority:** Canadian Centre for Cyber Security (CCCS)  
**Assessment Partners:** Standards Council of Canada (SCC) and accredited certification bodies  
**Industry:** Canadian defence and government supply chain  
**Current Program Status:** Level 1 launched April 2026; Levels 2 and 3 under development.

---

## 1. Overview

The **Canadian Program for Cyber Security Certification (CPCSC)** is a Government of Canada cybersecurity certification program designed to strengthen the security of Canadian defence contractors and suppliers.

The program establishes mandatory cybersecurity requirements for organizations that handle sensitive, unclassified Government of Canada information under applicable defence contracts.

Its primary objective is to ensure that organizations participating in Canada's defence supply chain implement appropriate security controls to protect government information against unauthorized access, disclosure, modification, disruption and cyberattacks.

The CPCSC is led by Public Services and Procurement Canada (PSPC), with support from the Department of National Defence (DND) and the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security (CCCS).

The program introduces three certification levels based on the sensitivity of information and the cybersecurity risk associated with government contracts.

### Main objectives

- Protect sensitive, unclassified federal government information.
- Strengthen cybersecurity across Canada's defence industrial base.
- Establish consistent security requirements for defence contractors and subcontractors.
- Reduce cybersecurity risks associated with third-party suppliers.
- Improve protection against unauthorized access and information disclosure.
- Support Canadian participation in international defence procurement.
- Align Canadian cybersecurity requirements with international standards and allied defence partners.

### Information protected by CPCSC

The program focuses on protecting **Specified Information (SI)** held by organizations outside the Government of Canada.

Specified Information may include:

- Sensitive, unclassified government information.
- Defence contract information not intended for public release.
- Controlled Goods information.
- Protected information identified by the applicable government authority.

The applicable contract determines which information must be protected and which certification level is required.

## 3. Certification Levels

The CPCSC consists of three certification levels.

Each level introduces different security requirements and assessment responsibilities according to the sensitivity of the information being handled.

### Level 1 – Basic Cybersecurity Requirements

**Purpose:** Establish fundamental cybersecurity practices to protect Specified Information against common cyber threats.

Level 1 is designed for organizations handling lower-risk sensitive government information.

Requirements:

- 13 cybersecurity controls.
- Annual cybersecurity self-assessment.
- Organizational attestation of compliance.
- Documented security practices.
- Retention of assessment results and supporting evidence.

Level 1 was launched on April 1, 2026.

The requirements are being introduced into selected defence contracts beginning in summer 2026.

The assessment focuses on fundamental cybersecurity practices, including account management, access control, authentication, device security, physical protection, network protection and security updates.

### Level 2 – Moderate Cybersecurity Requirements

**Purpose:** Provide stronger protection for organizations handling more sensitive defence information and performing higher-risk government contracts.

Level 2 introduces more extensive cybersecurity requirements and independent verification.

Program requirements:

- 98 cybersecurity controls.
- Independent external cybersecurity assessment.
- Assessment conducted by a certification body accredited by the Standards Council of Canada.
- Annual affirmation of compliance.
- Reassessment every three years, according to the published program model.

Level 2 is intended for contracts involving Controlled Defence Information and other more complex cyber-sensitive activities.

Its introduction into selected defence contracts is planned for spring 2027.

### Level 3 – Advanced Cybersecurity Requirements

**Purpose:** Protect highly sensitive defence information and critical systems associated with national defence.

Level 3 applies to the highest-risk defence contracts.

Program requirements:

- 130+ cybersecurity controls.
- Government-led cybersecurity assessment.
- Assessment conducted by the Department of National Defence.
- Annual affirmation of compliance.
- Reassessment every three years, according to the published program model.

Potential applications include:

- Weapon systems and military platforms.
- Critical infrastructure access.
- Highly sensitive defence information.
- Information shared with Five Eyes partners.
- High-risk defence technology and operational environments.

Level 3 is reserved for the most sensitive contractual environments.

**Important:** Levels 2 and 3 are still under development as of November 2026. Their final implementation details and assessment procedures should be confirmed through official Government of Canada publications and applicable contract requirements.

## 4. Level 1 – The 13 Security Controls

CPCSC Level 1 is based on 13 cybersecurity requirements from the Canadian industrial cybersecurity standard ITSP.10.171.

The controls are organized into six cybersecurity practice areas.

### 4.1 Access Control

**Control 1 – Account Management (03.01.01)**

Organizations must maintain control over user accounts and ensure that accounts are properly managed throughout their lifecycle.

Implementation:

- Maintain an inventory of user accounts.
- Create accounts through an approved provisioning process.
- Disable accounts when employees leave.
- Review accounts periodically.
- Avoid unnecessary shared and generic accounts.
- Maintain records of account modifications.

**Control 2 – Access Enforcement (03.01.02)**

Organizations must ensure users can only access information and systems they are authorized to use.

Implementation:

- Apply the principle of least privilege.
- Use role-based access control.
- Create security groups based on organizational responsibilities.
- Restrict administrative privileges.
- Review access permissions regularly.
- Remove unnecessary permissions.

**Control 3 – Use of External Systems (03.01.20)**

Organizations must control how external systems and services interact with organizational information.

Implementation:

- Identify approved systems and devices.
- Restrict unauthorized personal devices.
- Control access to external cloud services.
- Establish rules for remote access.
- Evaluate third-party services before use.
- Prevent unauthorized transfer of sensitive information.

**Control 4 – Publicly Accessible Content (03.01.22)**

Organizations must prevent unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information through publicly accessible systems.

Implementation:

- Review content before publication.
- Restrict publication privileges.
- Establish approval procedures for external communications.
- Review websites and public repositories.
- Prevent accidental publication of sensitive technical information.

### 4.2 Identification and Authentication

**Control 5 – User Identification and Authentication (03.05.01)**

Organizations must uniquely identify and authenticate individuals accessing their systems.

Implementation:

- Assign individual user accounts.
- Establish password security requirements.
- Prevent unauthorized account sharing.
- Implement appropriate authentication mechanisms.
- Configure automatic session locking.
- Establish procedures for account recovery.

**Control 6 – Device Identification and Authentication (03.05.02)**

Organizations must identify and authenticate devices before allowing them to connect to organizational systems.

Implementation:

- Maintain an authorized device inventory.
- Identify approved computers and mobile devices.
- Restrict unauthorized network connections.
- Implement device authentication.
- Remove unauthorized devices.
- Monitor network access.

**Control 7 – Multi-Factor Authentication (03.05.03)**

Organizations must implement multifactor authentication for privileged accounts and systems containing Specified Information.

Implementation:

- Enable MFA for administrative accounts.
- Require MFA for access to systems containing SI.
- Use authenticator applications or other approved authentication mechanisms.
- Establish secure MFA recovery procedures.
- Review authentication configurations regularly.

### 4.3 Media Protection

**Control 8 – Media Sanitization (03.08.03)**

Organizations must securely erase or destroy storage media before disposal or reuse.

Implementation:

- Sanitize hard drives and solid-state drives.
- Securely erase USB storage devices.
- Remove sensitive information from decommissioned computers.
- Maintain disposal records.
- Use approved destruction services when necessary.
- Document media sanitization procedures.

### 4.4 Physical Protection

**Control 9 – Physical Access Authorizations (03.10.01)**

Organizations must authorize and manage physical access to facilities and secure areas.

Implementation:

- Maintain a list of authorized personnel.
- Control access to server rooms and restricted areas.
- Manage employee access cards and keys.
- Remove physical access when employment ends.
- Review physical access permissions.

**Control 10 – Physical Access Control (03.10.07)**

Organizations must control and monitor physical access to facilities and equipment.

Implementation:

- Use physical locks and access control systems.
- Maintain visitor logs.
- Escort unauthorized visitors in restricted areas.
- Protect printed sensitive information.
- Secure server rooms and network equipment.
- Monitor physical access where appropriate.

### 4.5 System and Communications Protection

**Control 11 – Boundary Protection (03.13.01)**

Organizations must monitor and control communications at external and internal system boundaries.

Implementation:

- Deploy firewalls and network security devices.
- Restrict unnecessary inbound connections.
- Control outbound network traffic.
- Segment networks where appropriate.
- Separate public-facing systems from internal networks.
- Monitor network traffic.
- Document firewall and network configuration changes.

### 4.6 System and Information Integrity

**Control 12 – Flaw Remediation (03.14.01)**

Organizations must identify, report and correct security vulnerabilities and software flaws.

Implementation:

- Maintain operating system patching procedures.
- Apply security updates regularly.
- Prioritize critical vulnerabilities.
- Monitor vendor security advisories.
- Maintain patch management records.
- Verify successful deployment of security updates.

**Control 13 – Malicious Code Protection (03.14.02)**

Organizations must protect systems against malicious software and other malware threats.

Implementation:

- Deploy antivirus and endpoint protection.
- Maintain updated malware signatures and detection capabilities.
- Perform periodic security scans.
- Monitor endpoint security alerts.
- Investigate suspicious activity.
- Restrict unauthorized software execution where appropriate.

## 5. Technical Implementation

Organizations preparing for CPCSC certification should establish technical and administrative security measures that demonstrate the implementation of the applicable controls.

The following technologies can support implementation.

### Identity and Access Management

Examples:

- Microsoft Entra ID.
- Active Directory.
- Role-Based Access Control (RBAC).
- Privileged Identity Management (PIM).
- Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA).
- Conditional Access.

### Endpoint Security

Examples:

- Microsoft Intune.
- Microsoft Defender for Endpoint.
- BitLocker Drive Encryption.
- Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR).
- Windows Update for Business.
- Device compliance policies.

### Network Security

Examples:

- Cisco Meraki.
- Network firewalls.
- VLAN segmentation.
- Network Access Control (NAC).
- VPN technologies.
- Intrusion Detection and Prevention Systems (IDS/IPS).

### Information Protection

Examples:

- Microsoft Purview.
- Data Loss Prevention (DLP).
- Encryption at rest and in transit.
- Information classification.
- Secure file-sharing permissions.
- Access logging and auditing.

### Monitoring and Incident Detection

Examples:

- Microsoft Defender.
- Microsoft Sentinel.
- Security Information and Event Management (SIEM).
- Centralized logging.
- Security alert monitoring.
- Incident investigation and documentation.

**Important:** These technologies are examples of implementation solutions. CPCSC certification is based on satisfying applicable security requirements, not purchasing a particular software product.

## 6. Documentation and Evidence Requirements

Organizations must maintain sufficient documentation and evidence to demonstrate that their cybersecurity controls are implemented and operating effectively.

Examples of supporting documentation include:

| Document | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Information Security Policy | Defines organizational security responsibilities. |
| Access Control Policy | Establishes user access and authorization requirements. |
| Account Inventory | Documents organizational accounts and permissions. |
| Asset Inventory | Identifies authorized hardware and software. |
| Acceptable Use Policy | Defines acceptable use of company systems. |
| MFA Policy | Establishes authentication requirements. |
| Network Security Documentation | Describes network boundaries and protections. |
| Patch Management Policy | Defines vulnerability and update procedures. |
| Incident Response Plan | Establishes incident handling procedures. |
| Media Sanitization Policy | Defines secure media disposal procedures. |
| Physical Security Policy | Establishes facility access requirements. |
| System Security Plan (SSP) | Describes the system, security environment and implemented controls. |
| Assessment Records | Documents assessment results and supporting evidence. |

Evidence may include:

- System configuration screenshots.
- Access control reports.
- Authentication configuration records.
- Firewall configurations.
- Endpoint protection reports.
- Security event logs.
- Vulnerability assessment results.
- Employee training records.
- Hardware disposal certificates.
- Approved security procedures.

Documentation should reflect the actual security configuration and operating procedures of the organization.

## 7. Implementation Strategy

Organizations preparing for CPCSC should follow a structured implementation process.

### Phase 1 – Identify Information and Scope

- Identify government information handled by the organization.
- Determine where Specified Information is stored.
- Identify systems, applications and devices that process this information.
- Identify employees and third parties with access.
- Determine contractual cybersecurity requirements.

### Phase 2 – Perform a Security Gap Assessment

- Review applicable CPCSC controls.
- Identify existing security measures.
- Compare current practices against required controls.
- Identify missing or ineffective safeguards.
- Prioritize remediation based on risk.

### Phase 3 – Implement Security Controls

- Establish identity and access management.
- Implement MFA.
- Secure endpoints and mobile devices.
- Deploy network security controls.
- Establish patch management procedures.
- Implement information protection measures.
- Secure physical facilities.

### Phase 4 – Develop Documentation

- Create security policies.
- Document technical configurations.
- Maintain asset and account inventories.
- Develop incident response procedures.
- Establish evidence collection procedures.
- Document control ownership and responsibilities.

### Phase 5 – Conduct Security Assessment

- Perform internal control reviews.
- Examine system configurations.
- Interview relevant personnel.
- Test implemented security mechanisms.
- Document findings and remediation activities.

### Phase 6 – Certification and Continuous Compliance

- Complete the applicable certification assessment.
- Retain assessment documentation.
- Submit required certification information.
- Maintain security controls.
- Review security policies regularly.
- Complete annual affirmations or self-assessments as required.
- Prepare for subsequent reassessments.

## 8. Relationship Between CPCSC, CMMC and NIST

CPCSC is Canada's domestic cybersecurity certification program for applicable defence suppliers.

It was developed with significant alignment to the U.S. Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC) framework.

Both programs use NIST-based technical cybersecurity controls.

| Framework | Country | Primary Application |
|---|---|---|
| CPCSC | Canada | Canadian defence contractors and suppliers. |
| CMMC | United States | U.S. Department of Defense contractors. |
| NIST SP 800-171 | United States | Protection of Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) in nonfederal systems. |
| NIST SP 800-172 | United States | Enhanced security requirements for protecting CUI against advanced persistent threats. |
| ITSP.10.171 | Canada | Protection of Specified Information in non-Government of Canada systems and organizations. |

### CPCSC and NIST SP 800-171

The Canadian industrial cybersecurity standard ITSP.10.171 provides the underlying security requirements for protecting Specified Information.

Its technical requirements are aligned with the NIST SP 800-171 family of security controls.

The Canadian assessment methodology for Level 1 is based on NIST SP 800-171A Revision 3.

### CPCSC and CMMC

CMMC is administered by the U.S. Department of Defense, while CPCSC is administered by the Government of Canada.

The two programs are separate certification systems, despite their technical alignment.

The Government of Canada may recognize valid CMMC certification on a case-by-case basis after confirming that the assessment covers the required scope.

Canada may also verify compliance with specific requirements when necessary.

Organizations should not automatically assume that CMMC certification guarantees CPCSC certification.

Organizations should maintain a compliance mapping to identify overlapping requirements and avoid unnecessary duplication.

## 11. Recommended Security Practices

Organizations preparing for CPCSC should establish a sustainable cybersecurity program rather than treating certification as a one-time exercise.

Recommended practices include:

1. Maintain accurate hardware and software inventories.
2. Implement centralized identity and access management.
3. Enforce MFA for privileged access.
4. Apply least-privilege permissions.
5. Encrypt sensitive information.
6. Maintain secure backups.
7. Establish vulnerability and patch management.
8. Monitor network and endpoint security.
9. Implement incident response procedures.
10. Maintain security documentation.
11. Conduct periodic access reviews.
12. Review third-party service providers.
13. Train employees on cybersecurity responsibilities.
14. Perform regular internal security assessments.

## 13. Conclusion

The Canadian Program for Cyber Security Certification establishes a standardized approach to protecting sensitive government information throughout Canada's defence supply chain.

Through its three certification levels, CPCSC introduces progressively stronger cybersecurity requirements based on organizational responsibilities, information sensitivity and contractual risk.

For Canadian defence suppliers, implementing CPCSC involves more than deploying technical security products. Organizations must establish appropriate policies, access controls, monitoring capabilities, documentation practices and continuous security assessments.

CPCSC also provides Canadian organizations with a domestic certification framework aligned with international cybersecurity standards and allied defence requirements.

**The central objective is to ensure that Canadian defence suppliers can securely handle government information and maintain the cybersecurity resilience of Canada's defence industrial base.**
